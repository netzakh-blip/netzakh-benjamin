Critical Theory • Discussion Reply

Freud, Marx, and the Corpse in the Bedroom

A reply to a class discussion on mixing psychoanalytic and Marxist criticism, giving me the chance to talk about my beloved weirdo Wilhelm Reich, who wanders in schlepping the strangest possible luggage.

The question

“Do you think combining Marxist and psychoanalytic approaches to a story like ‘The Yellow Wallpaper’ might provide a more complete interpretation than either theory could provide independently?”

I like where you're going with this buddy, especially the idea that a conflict that looks intensely private can still have material machinery humming behind it. I agree with the basic move. I'm a little suspicious, though, of calling the result a “more complete” interpretation. We can keep bolting theoretical lenses onto a story forever until the poor thing resembles an optometrist's nightmare. The interesting question is whether the theories actually explain one another's blind spots.

Marxism and psychoanalysis also aren't simply two versions of the same theory. Marx predates Freud, obviously, but chronology isn't really the problem. Causation is. Freud begins with the psyche, childhood, family, repression, unconscious desire, fear, sexuality, and all the strange private machinery we drag behind us without quite knowing it's there. Marxism begins somewhere else: material relations, class, ideology, institutions, and history.

Tyson puts the distinction almost perfectly: “psychoanalysis focuses on the individual psyche and its formation within the family,” while Marxism focuses on “the material/historical forces” that shape psychological experience and behaviour (64). She goes further: for Marxism, even the family isn't the original source of identity because “both the individual and the family are products of material/historical circumstances” (64).

That seems more interesting to me than simply saying one theory explains the inside and the other explains the outside. Tyson's point is nastier than that. The outside gets inside. The family may produce psychological wounds, but Marxism asks who built the family structure, supplied its ideology, defined respectability, distributed money and authority, and decided whose desires could safely be spoken in the first place. Psychoanalysis then asks what happens after all of that gets buried in somebody's skull.

I can't quite reduce Freud to “everything is sex,” tempting as that caricature is. Sexuality certainly carries an enormous amount of explanatory weight in Freud, especially given the ferociously repressive bourgeois culture he was writing inside, but Tyson makes an important qualification. Freud “didn't stop” at sexuality as biological pressure, and contemporary psychoanalysis treats sexuality as bound up with identity, childhood, pleasure, fear, and the formation of the self. Her most obvious sentence is that sexuality “is not a matter of biological drive-discharge mechanisms but a matter of meanings” (24).

Still, the tension with Marx is real. If unconscious desire and repression explain why humans behave as they do, where does material history sit? If material relations and ideology produce the family and its values, how autonomous can the Freudian psyche really be? People have been fighting over that corpse for a century.

Enter Wilhelm Reich, shpritzing the gasoline all over the room

“Weirdo” Wilhelm Reich is probably the most entertaining answer to the question at hand because he tried to weld the two systems together instead of politely letting them occupy neighbouring seminar rooms. The early Reich's problem was basically this: Marxism could explain exploitation rather well, but it had a much harder time explaining why exploited people might emotionally desire authoritarianism, obey institutions that hurt them, or defend a social order contrary to their apparent material interests. Freud had tools for repression and unconscious attachment. Marx had tools for class and ideology. Reich wanted the whole damned machine.

Repression becomes historical. The authoritarian family isn't merely where an individual's private neurosis happens; it can become one of the places where a social order reproduces itself psychologically. Class society doesn't just control wages and property. It can crawl into desire, shame, sexuality, obedience, and the child's relation to authority. Marx explains the architecture. Freud explains some of the ghosts living in it. Reich asks why the tenants sometimes defend the landlord.

I have a special affection for Reich, beyond our shared kinship. I've read nearly everything he wrote, and I once built an orgone accumulator for one of my ex-wives, who was a true believer in his later theories. I am admittedly approaching this particular theoretical disaster with exactly 0 safe academic distance. (Not that I ever come with much of it anyhow).

The theory eventually got considerably wackier.

So what would this actually do to “A Rose for Emily”?

Take one thing: Homer Barron's corpse.

Psychoanalytically, Emily's murder and preservation of Homer can be read as an obscene defence against abandonment. Her father dominates her emotional and romantic life, drives away potential suitors, and then dies. Emily famously resists even that loss. Homer threatens another departure. Murder solves the unbearable psychic problem with monstrous efficiency: the beloved can no longer leave, reject, change, or desire anything beyond her. Desire becomes possession.

The Marxist question is where Emily learned this grammar of possession in the first place. She is the surviving relic of a Southern aristocratic order built around inherited status, property, patriarchy, racial hierarchy, and the conviction that the Grierson name exempts her from ordinary social obligations. Homer is a Northern labouring man, socially beneath the aristocratic identity Emily has inherited. Her private catastrophe therefore happens inside a class order already dying around her.

A Freudo-Marxist reading joins those two without pretending they're identical. Emily's terror of abandonment is psychological, but the form her desire takes is historically furnished. She has learned a world in which status is inherited, authority is patriarchal, and possession is a fundamental social relation. She cannot preserve the old South, her father, her class position, or Homer alive. So she preserves Homer dead.

The corpse becomes both lover and property. The sealed bedroom is simultaneously an unconscious chamber and a mausoleum for a class order. Emily doesn't merely refuse Homer's departure. She refuses historical dispossession itself, turning erotic attachment into literal ownership when every other form of ownership and authority around her is collapsing.

And that, I think, is where the suggestion really works. Tyson explicitly says that psychoanalytic critics examine “family conflicts and psychological wounds,” while Marxist critics can examine the same behaviour as a product of ideological forces and even show how family dysfunctions are produced by socioeconomic systems (64). A Rose for Emily is almost indecently suited to that collision. The corpse in the bedroom can be both a symptom of private repression and the grotesque final property of a dying aristocratic consciousness. We don't have to choose.

I'd make the same move with The Yellow Wallpaper. Psychoanalysis can read repression, projection, desire, and psychic fragmentation. Marxism can ask who owns the room, who controls the money, whose labour counts as work, why medical authority belongs to the husband, and what material conditions make the narrator's enforced idleness possible. The theories can synergize together when one can explain how a social order gets translated into private suffering. Otherwise we're just collecting lenses of various perscriptions.

Messy.

But that's probably the point. Reich's most useful contribution here isn't that he finally reconciled Freud and Marx. He didn't. It's that he refused to let either theory escape the question the other made uncomfortable: how does history get inside a person, and why do people sometimes come to desire the very arrangements that constrain them?
