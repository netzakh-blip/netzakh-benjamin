The Strike Before the Myth

Ratner Bros., IWW Local 59, and the Jewish garment workers who tried an IWW organisation in the needle trades before labour history paved over it.

By Netzakh · Netzakh.space · Dispatch 04 September 2026
A later New York garment strike fills the street. The famous explosion came in 1909. Local 59 had already been testing the fuse in 1907. Image: Labor Arts.
The problem
Most accounts hurry toward 1909. Local 59 was already striking in the garment trades in 1907.

What the scraps show
In 1907 Jewish garment workers were choosing among rival unions with different programmes. Local 59 put revolutionary industrial unionism inside that fight.

Open the histories of New York's Jewish needle trades and 1907 goes by fast. Local 59 can vanish in a paragraph. Ratner Bros. gets less. Yet the surviving notices give us twelve weeks of strike, arrests, an IWW local and a named employer. Then the paper trail thins out.

The familiar New York sequence runs through sweatshops, Yiddish socialism, Clara Lemlich, the Uprising of the 20,000, the Great Revolt, Triangle and the ILGWU. Read forward from 1907, though, and the line breaks apart. Workers were choosing among organisations whose later reputations tell us very little about how plausible they looked at the time.

In 1907, before the famous uprising, Jewish garment workers were already striking under the IWW.

The IWW was barely two years old. Later memory parks it among Western miners, lumber camps, free-speech fights and Joe Hill. New York's records put it somewhere less cinematic: among pressers and sewing machines. Local 59 belonged to the needle trades.

By 1906 several New York IWW garment locals had combined as Local 59. Later Jewish trade-union histories remembered several branches, including the white-goods workers, while the Industrial Union Bulletin recorded branch activity as it happened. The local fought employers and rival union organisations. It lasted long enough to leave records in more than one archive.

Then somebody walked out at Ratner Bros.

The surviving account is thin. On 17 August 1907 an IWW report put the New York white-goods strike in its tenth week; several strikers had been arrested and released without conviction. A later notice says it ended after twelve weeks. The retrospective Jewish-union history gives the same duration: “the ‘white goods’ workers went on strike in 1907 for twelve weeks.” That is nearly three months compressed into a line.

The IWW yearbook supplies the employer: Ratner Bros. For twelve weeks its workers held a strike through an organisation founded on the proposition that workers should organise by industry and replace the wage system itself.

The notices give a duration, arrests and an end date. I have not yet found a reliable account of the original demands or final settlement. Calling the strike a victory or defeat would outrun the evidence.

Two years before the Uprising of the 20,000, then, white-goods workers in New York sustained a twelve-week IWW strike. Ratner was one fight among several. Local 59 included cloak pressers associated with Morris Sigman; members also struck R. Sadowsky & Co. and Beller & Co. A later union history says an injunction broke the Beller strike.

In 1907 Jewish garment workers were still fighting over what kind of union movement they would build.

At Ratner the disagreement was practical. What could workers demand from a union, and who got to run it? New York's Jewish workers were answering that question through rival organisations, dues cards and strikes.

The AFL and garment-union traditions put considerable weight on durable trade organisation, bargaining machinery and agreements. The IWW called for industrial organisation across craft lines and treated the union as preparation for workers' control beyond capitalism. Printed programmes drew hard lines. People crossed them. Organisers changed affiliations, unions swallowed branches, and a worker facing the foreman on Monday had problems no manifesto could postpone.

Morris Sigman changes uniforms

Morris Sigman is where the labels start leaking. Cornell's ILGWU records identify him as an organiser of the Independent Cloak Pressers' Union and a founder of the IWW. The Jewish-union history places the Industrial Cloak Pressers' Union among the organisations that became Local 59 and names Sigman as its leader.

Sigman later became president of the ILGWU, serving from 1923 to 1928.

By 1923 Sigman was president of the ILGWU. He helped build the IWW and later ran the ILGWU. Those unions fought each other. Sigman belonged to both, at different moments.

Local 59 disappeared as an independent force. Some of its people moved elsewhere. On 31 December 1908, Branch 6 formally dissolved into ILGWU Local 35. Sigman's later career makes the same movement visible at the level of leadership. What looks retrospectively like a dead branch was partly folded into institutions that survived it.

The twelve weeks before 1909

Ratner sits in 1907, before the later map of the needle trades had settled. Workers could encounter the IWW, AFL unions, socialist organisations, anarchist circles and mutual-aid bodies in the same immigrant labour world.

Progressive Era Jewish radicalism was crowded. Socialist parties, anarchists, Wobblies, craft unions, mutual-aid societies and Yiddish newspapers competed for many of the same workers. Their disagreements concerned strategy, authority, class politics and the shape of a labour movement they wanted. Ratner gives that argument a shop and a date.

The ILGWU became a major institution; Local 59 disappeared. The mass strikes of 1909 and 1910 then generated a much larger documentary and commemorative record. Ratner sits just before them, easy to overlook precisely because later events were larger and better preserved.

Almost.

Ratner lasted twelve weeks. Strikers were arrested. Local 59 had branches and an industrial programme. This happened before the mass shirtwaist revolt made 1909 the familiar starting point for a generation of garment militancy. The Ratner record is incomplete. It still gives us a stubborn fact: in 1907, workers in New York's garment industry were already testing an IWW route that would soon narrow, merge and largely disappear from the standard story.

Ratner Bros., 1907: twelve weeks on strike before the famous garment uprisings began.

Research note. This public-facing essay is deliberately narrower than the underlying research project. The surviving online summaries establish Local 59, its branches, the twelve-week white-goods/Ratner strike, arrests during the action, other IWW garment strikes, and Sigman’s organisational career. A fuller scholarly treatment should return to the 1907 Industrial Union Bulletin, contemporary Yiddish labour press, employer records where available, and the ILGWU material to reconstruct demands, participants and the settlement or collapse of the Ratner action without pretending the archive says more than it does.
