 valued freedom because it protected his clients from financial loss, and it did not lead Benjamin to challenge slavery in Louisiana.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-25"><a aria-label="Footnote 25" href="#fn-25">25</a></sup></p>
<p>Benjamin soon acquired Belle Chasse, a sugar plantation below New Orleans, and enslaved more than one hundred people at its height.  Sugar production required dangerous mill work, organized labor gangs, credit, and access to global markets.  Benjamin’s ownership brought wealth and planter status, then financial risk when crop failures and changing circumstances led him to sell the estate in the 1850s.  Claims that he treated enslaved workers humanely reproduced the standards of paternalist memory rather than evaluating ownership itself.  He chose to buy people, direct their labor, and use their market value to enter Louisiana’s ruling class.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-26"><a aria-label="Footnote 26" href="#fn-26">26</a></sup></p>
<p>Law converted that coercion into ordinary transactions.  Benjamin represented owners in disputes involving plantations and enslaved people, and he defended a Louisiana apportionment rule that counted each enslaved person fully for representation while denying them votes.  The arrangement enlarged the political power of slaveholding districts through the bodies of people excluded from politics.  His later <em>Treatise on the Law of Sale</em> defined sale as the transfer of general property for a monetary price.  The technical definition appeared neutral, but Benjamin’s American practice had operated where courts included human beings within the category of saleable things.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-27"><a aria-label="Footnote 27" href="#fn-27">27</a></sup></p>
<p>Benjamin entered the Louisiana legislature as a Whig in 1842 and used commercial alliances to expand his political base.  John and Thomas Slidell connected him to powerful clients, while his command of constitutional detail made him valuable at state conventions.  Louisiana’s plural society did not remove prejudice, but it created room for a talented Jewish lawyer who accepted its racial and property rules.  Benjamin’s rise demonstrated neither perfect tolerance nor a simple surrender of identity.  It showed how whiteness, wealth, and regional loyalty could admit a religious outsider to mastery.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-28"><a aria-label="Footnote 28" href="#fn-28">28</a></sup></p>
<h2 class="section-title" id="the-senate-and-the-nationalization-of-slave-property">The Senate and the Nationalization of Slave Property</h2>
<p>The Louisiana legislature elected Benjamin to the United States Senate in 1852.  Florida’s David Levy Yulee had entered the chamber earlier, so Benjamin did not hold an uncontested claim as the first Jewish senator.  Yulee and Benjamin both descended from Caribbean Sephardic families and both married Christian women, but contemporaries more consistently identified Benjamin with Jewishness.  The distinction should not obscure the larger achievement: a poor immigrant’s son entered one of the highest offices in the nation without converting.  His importance, however, depended less on crossing that barrier than on what he did after crossing it.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-29"><a aria-label="Footnote 29" href="#fn-29">29</a></sup></p>
<p>The collapse of the Whig Party pushed Benjamin toward the Democrats as Republicans organized against slavery’s expansion.  Lower South politicians joined plantation agriculture to territorial growth, federal protection, and international commerce.  Historian Brian Schoen showed that cotton leaders did not defend an isolated premodern order; they pursued a global economic system that linked enslaved labor to banks, shipping, British manufacturing, and western land.  Benjamin’s commercial nationalism fit that project until Republican victory threatened slave property.  He changed parties in 1856 and became one of the Senate’s most learned defenders of slavery.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-30"><a aria-label="Footnote 30" href="#fn-30">30</a></sup></p>
<p>The territorial controversy forced Benjamin to define the source of enslavement.  Antislavery constitutionalists argued that slavery existed only where local positive law created it, which meant freedom prevailed in federal territories unless valid legislation established bondage.  Proslavery lawyers answered that the Constitution recognized enslaved people as property and required federal protection wherever an owner traveled.  James Oakes described the Republican strategy as a “cordon of freedom” designed to contain slavery and place it on a course toward extinction.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-31"><a aria-label="Footnote 31" href="#fn-31">31</a></sup>  Benjamin understood containment as an attack on both property and Southern political equality.</p>
<p>His March 1858 Kansas speech made the position explicit.  Proslavery delegates had produced the Lecompton Constitution amid fraud and competing territorial governments, and President James Buchanan asked Congress to admit Kansas under it.  Benjamin defended admission, denied that statutes alone created slavery, and used the Supreme Court’s <em>Dred Scott</em> ruling to claim constitutional protection for slave property in the territories.  He called the defense of property a sacred constitutional obligation.  Learned history and legal craft thus converted one person’s mastery of another into a right that the national government had to enforce.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-32"><a aria-label="Footnote 32" href="#fn-32">32</a></sup></p>
<p>Antisemitic attacks did not remove Benjamin from the circle of white authority.  Ohio Senator Benjamin Wade called him an “Israelite with Egyptian principles,” using the Exodus against a Jewish defender of slavery.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-33"><a aria-label="Footnote 33" href="#fn-33">33</a></sup>  Wade exposed a moral contradiction but also reduced a political opponent to ancestry.  Benjamin answered as a Southern senator rather than through Jewish theology, and the record did not establish that scripture produced his proslavery constitutionalism.  His experience showed that a person could suffer religious prejudice while exercising legal power over people denied citizenship altogether.</p>
<h2 class="section-title" id="secession-and-a-confederate-place-of-belonging">Secession and a Confederate Place of Belonging</h2>
<p>Republican victory in 1860 turned Benjamin’s property argument into a theory of secession.  Most Republicans promised no immediate federal abolition inside the slave states, but they opposed expansion and expected containment to weaken slavery over time.  Benjamin treated that future as an intolerable loss of equality.  In his December 31 Senate speech, he argued that the people of each state had retained the sovereign authority to withdraw from the constitutional compact.  His legal reasoning presented Louisiana’s departure as a remedy for violated rights, yet the right he placed in greatest danger concerned ownership in people.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-34"><a aria-label="Footnote 34" href="#fn-34">34</a></sup></p>
<p>Benjamin’s February 4 farewell joined grievance to prophecy.  He denied Republican claims of moderation, warned that coercion would produce war, and departed with Louisiana’s other senator, John Slidell.  His action carried an additional meaning within Jewish history: the son of one Charleston religious secessionist now endorsed national disunion.  That parallel did not establish a causal inheritance, since the Reformed Society sought liberty within American government while the Confederacy protected slavery against an electoral result.  It instead exposed how the language of conscience and self-government could serve radically different moral purposes.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-35"><a aria-label="Footnote 35" href="#fn-35">35</a></sup></p>
<p>The Confederate constitution resolved any ambiguity about those purposes.  It prohibited laws that impaired “the right of property in negro slaves” and required territorial government to protect that property.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-36"><a aria-label="Footnote 36" href="#fn-36">36</a></sup>  Benjamin accepted appointment as the Confederacy’s first attorney general on February 25, 1861, and President Jefferson Davis later moved him to the War Department and then the State Department.  Davis valued his discipline, memory, legal judgment, and capacity for sustained work.  Benjamin’s cabinet membership marked unprecedented political inclusion for a Jew in North America, but the government used his talent to make Black exclusion a constitutional foundation.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-37"><a aria-label="Footnote 37" href="#fn-37">37</a></sup></p>
<p>Early Confederate admirers treated Benjamin’s appointment as proof of Southern tolerance.  No religious test barred him, and antisemitic criticism did not prevent his service at the center of government.  But the celebration converted inclusion into an alibi by asking readers to judge the Confederacy through one Jewish official rather than through four million enslaved people.  Robert Rosen’s <em>The Jewish Confederates</em> recovered many neglected Jewish soldiers and civilians, yet its celebratory passages sometimes approached this logic.  Benjamin’s office demonstrated that a hierarchical society could broaden its white ruling class without surrendering hierarchy.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-38"><a aria-label="Footnote 38" href="#fn-38">38</a></sup></p>
<h2 class="section-title" id="jewish-americans-in-a-divided-war">Jewish Americans in a Divided War</h2>
<p>Benjamin did not represent a unified Jewish politics during the Civil War.  Jewish Americans served in both armies, defended both governments, and argued over slavery, emancipation, and loyalty much as other white Americans did.  Southern congregations often supported their states through prayer, relief work, and military service, while northern Jews enlisted for the Union and contested federal discrimination.  The variety defeated any claim that Jewish memory of Exodus automatically produced abolitionism or that Southern acceptance dictated Confederate loyalty.  Religion supplied languages and communities, but local ties, family, class, and political conviction shaped wartime choices.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-39"><a aria-label="Footnote 39" href="#fn-39">39</a></sup></p><figure class="archive-figure portrait"><img alt="Portrait of Private Edwin Francis Jemison of the 2nd Louisiana Regiment, Confederate States Army." data-remote="true" decoding="async" loading="lazy" referrerpolicy="no-referrer" src="https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/cwp/4a40000/4a40900/4a40928r.jpg"/><div class="image-fallback">The archive image could not be loaded inside this preview. <a href="https://www.loc.gov/item/2018667161/" rel="noopener" target="_blank">Open the source image.</a></div><figcaption class="archive-caption"><strong>A Louisiana soldier.</strong> Pvt. <p>Secession made Benjamin a subject of Jewish press debate as well as national politics.  A January 1861 <em>Boston Transcript</em> editorial used Benjamin, David Yulee, and Charleston donor Benjamin Mordecai to blame an allegedly homeless and ungrateful Jewish people for disunion.  Cincinnati rabbi and editor Isaac Mayer Wise answered that “the Jew is a man, an independent man” who held the same right to political judgment as any other citizen.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-40"><a aria-label="Footnote 40" href="#fn-40">40</a></sup>  Wise opposed abolitionism and did not defend Benjamin’s proslavery program, but he refused collective religious blame.  He explained that Benjamin and Yulee remained Jewish brethren in religion while they represented mixed political constituencies rather than American Jews.  The exchange distinguished equal citizenship from agreement with the purposes for which a citizen used it.</p>
<p>The Jonas family illustrated this division within Benjamin’s own extended network.  Abraham Jonas, a Kentucky-born Jewish lawyer and friend of Abraham Lincoln, raised sons who chose opposing armies.  Four served the Confederacy, while one entered Union service; Lincoln allowed a captured Confederate son to visit his dying father.  Benjamin F. Jonas, later known as Frank, fought in a Louisiana battery after practicing law in New Orleans.  The family’s divided service made the Civil War a conflict inside Jewish households rather than a simple encounter between Jews and an outside nation.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-41"><a aria-label="Footnote 41" href="#fn-41">41</a></sup></p>
<p>Primary evidence also revealed political combinations that later memory flattened.  In 1857, Benjamin F. Jonas wrote Lincoln about John Shelby, a free Black man jailed in New Orleans for lacking proof of status, and thanked the Illinois lawyer for helping secure evidence of Shelby’s freedom.  Jonas later served the Confederacy and then helped restore white Democratic power in Louisiana.  His earlier intervention did not erase those commitments, but it showed that legal aid to one Black claimant could coexist with support for a racial system.  The pattern resembled Judah Benjamin’s <em>Creole</em> argument: an advocate could recognize a specific freedom claim without attacking slavery as an institution.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-42"><a aria-label="Footnote 42" href="#fn-42">42</a></sup></p>
<p>Penina Moïse offered a different connection to Charleston Reform.  The poet and educator wrote nearly two hundred hymns for Beth Elohim, directed its religious school, and gave an American Jewish woman an unusual public role in worship.  Her hymn “Our Country” declared liberty native to humanity, while her Passover verse celebrated release from bondage.  During secession, however, she wrote “Cockades of Blue” in praise of South Carolina and spent the war within the Confederacy.  Her work placed universal religious freedom beside Confederate nationalism and showed that the contradiction extended beyond Benjamin.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-43"><a aria-label="Footnote 43" href="#fn-43">43</a></sup></p>
<p>Southern Jewish worship also adapted republican language to the new Confederate nation.  At the May 1862 dedication of a Montgomery synagogue, New Orleans rabbi James K. Gutheim asked God to protect “our beloved country, the Confederate States of America” and bless “a cause which we conceive to be just.”<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-44"><a aria-label="Footnote 44" href="#fn-44">44</a></sup>  His prayer joined Jewish law, universal human brotherhood, Confederate independence, and military victory within one service.  It did not establish Benjamin’s private belief, and Gutheim did not speak for every Southern Jew.  It did show how a Jewish civic vocabulary once directed toward membership in the United States could legitimize a slaveholding republic.</p>
<p>Northern events revealed another limit on Jewish belonging.  Major General Ulysses S. Grant’s General Orders Number 11 expelled “Jews as a class” from his military department in December 1862, and President Abraham Lincoln revoked the order after Jewish leaders protested.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-45"><a aria-label="Footnote 45" href="#fn-45">45</a></sup>  Congress also changed military chaplain rules that had initially excluded rabbis.  These controversies proved that the United States did not offer uncomplicated religious equality even as it moved toward emancipation.  They also underscored the difference between reversible discrimination against Jewish citizens and the hereditary enslavement that federal armies began to destroy.<sup class="fn-ref" id="fnref-46"><a aria-label="Footnote 46" href="#fn-46">46</a></sup></p>
<h2 class="section-title" id="building-and-defending-the-slaveholders-republic">Building and Defending the Slaveholders’ Republic</h2>
<p>Benjamin’s movement through three cabinet posts followed the Confederacy’s growing crisis.  As attorney general, he issued opinions and helped organize a government created with few administrative precedents.  As secretary of war, he confronted shortages, competing state authorities, and military reverses.  Davis protected him after criticism of the Roanoke Island defeat, and Benjamin resigned from the War Department before entering the more durable role of secretary of state in March 1862.
